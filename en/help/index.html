How to use the Nomad360 business calculator

You have a business idea. First work out what it costs to make or deliver, what price you need, and whether the whole business can carry that price. Then ask: who will buy this from me at this price, why, and where? What value must it offer, and how should it be packaged?

The calculator handles the money side. It does not find customers.

Pick your route

  • I'm planning my own business.
    Read H01–H11 and H17, then the cotton tote and US store examples.
  • I advise business owners.
    Read H12–H16, then the consultant example.

Get your numbers ready

  • What you sell: a product or a service.
  • What it costs to buy, make or deliver: materials, purchases, work, logistics.
  • Your advertising plan: budget, cost per click, expected conversions.
  • Shared payments: rent, staff, equipment, certificates.
  • Taxes that apply to you, and the rates you will type in.
  • Your own money, or loan terms.
  • The currency you work in, and any foreign-currency payments.

For each assumption, write down in your own notes where it came from and how sure you are. The calculator does not store that.

H01. V1 or V2: where do I start?

Start with V1 to price one product or one service. Use V2 when you sell several things that share costs.

V1

V1 models one product or one service. The first screen asks “What are you selling?” and sends you down a route:

  • Product: you make it, you buy it to resell, or you dropship it.
  • Offline service.
  • Online service: you do the work, you hire an executor, or you act as an agent.

An online service can be calculated in a simple or a full version. That is how detailed V1 gets. It has nothing to do with V2.

Linked V2

If you priced several products in V1, you can carry them into V2. Linked V2 keeps what V1 already worked out: price limits, discount, tax and service terms. Use it to add shared costs and see the whole business.

Standalone V2

Standalone V2 is a separate portfolio with its own draft. You enter each item (SKU), its advertising, how it is sourced (own production, resale or dropshipping), batch sizes, stock, shared resources, a month-by-month cash flow and starting capital. It does not read your V1 work.

Your first run

  1. Open the calculator and answer “What are you selling?”
  2. Fill in the screens in order. Fields that need fixing are marked.
  3. Read the result, change one assumption, and compare.
  4. Go back to the first screen from the menu whenever you need to.

Want to see a finished case first? See Example A (V1) and Example B (V2).

H02. What goes into cost?

Cost is what you pay to produce or deliver what you sell. Enter it the way you will actually pay it.

  • You make it: materials, work, packaging, delivery of materials, and allowance for rejects.
  • You buy it to resell: the purchase price plus delivery to you.
  • You dropship: the supplier's price on each order. You hold no stock.
  • Offline or online service: your own time or the executor's fee, tools, platform fees, and anything else the service needs.
  • You are an agent: your income is your agreed fee, not the whole sale. Who pays which costs depends on your contract with the principal.

Avoid counting twice

Do not enter a cost in a product and again as a shared cost. Pick one place. Also keep apart two ideas that look alike: the money you pay for a batch, and the cost of the goods you have actually sold.

Example (illustrative scenario)

You pay a supplier 600 for a batch of 10 units. That is 60 per unit. You sell 2.

The 600 left your account before the sales. The cost of goods sold is 2 × 60 = 120. Eight units, worth 480, are still in stock.

If you pay a supplier in another currency, see H09.

H03. How do I read price, discount and margin?

Start from your costs and the profit you want, and the calculator shows the price you need. Whether customers accept that price is a separate question (see H17).

Margin is not markup

Example (illustrative scenario)

Cost 100. Selling price 125, before any pass-through tax.

Margin = (125 − 100) ÷ 125 = 20%. Markup = (125 − 100) ÷ 100 = 25%.

Margin divides by the price. Markup divides by the cost. Mixing them up is the most common pricing mistake.

What each version shows

  • V1 gives a price range, with a target and a minimum. You choose the discount you are prepared to give.
  • Linked V2 inherits those V1 limits and discounts.
  • Standalone V2 shows a base price and the margin you actually get, including the advertising cost per customer.

The price the buyer pays can include a tax that passes through to the state. That tax is not your revenue (H08).

A product's margin before shared costs is not the profit of the whole business. Shared costs come next (H05).

H04. How are customers and acquisition cost forecast?

You enter an advertising budget and how people behave at each step. The calculator turns that into expected customers and a cost per customer. These are your assumptions, not measured results.

The funnel

Budget → cost per click (CPC) → clicks → leads → orders. If you enter the click-through rate (CTR), you also get impressions.

Example (illustrative scenario)

  • Budget 1,000, CPC 2 → 500 clicks.
  • 10% of clicks become leads → 50 leads.
  • 20% of leads order → 10 orders.
  • Advertising cost per customer = 1,000 ÷ 10 = 100.
  • With a 5% CTR, 500 clicks need 10,000 impressions.

What you will see

  • Cost per customer. The calculator also shows a weighted figure for portfolios. It spreads the shared budget across items. It does not mean each order really cost that much.
  • ROAS (revenue ÷ ad spend, shown as ×) appears only where the branch calculates it.

These numbers can change the price you need, because advertising cost is part of cost. A forecast funnel is not proof of demand.

H05. How do V2 portfolios and shared costs work?

V2 lets you price several items and the costs they share, in one model.

Items (SKUs) and mix

  • The share of sales in units must add up to 100%.
  • The share of revenue can be different, because items have different prices.
  • Shared advertising is divided by each item's share of revenue. You can also add a campaign for a single item.

Shared resources

A warehouse, an employee, a rented space or a certificate can serve several items. Enter it once. Choose which items it serves, how often you pay (monthly or one time), and, where the branch asks for it, how much capacity it has and how much each item uses. The result shows which items each cost is assigned to.

Example (illustrative scenario)

Rent of 300 a month serves two items. The business pays 300, not 600. However the model splits it between the two items, the parts must add up to 300.

The split is analysis. It does not push each shared cost into the cost of a single item.

H06. Why is profit not the same as cash?

Profit says whether a sale earned more than it cost. Cash says whether you can pay your bills this month. A profitable business can still run out of money.

Example (illustrative scenario)

Month 1: you pay a supplier 600 for 10 units. Cash: −600.

Month 2: you sell 2 units at 100 each, before tax. Revenue 200. Cost of goods sold 120. Gross profit 80.

Cash after month 2: −600 + 200 = −400. The profit is real, and you are still 400 short.

Rules of thumb

  • A loan you receive is money in, not a sale.
  • Paying back the loan's principal is money out, not an operating expense.
  • Prepayments and stock purchases leave your account before the sales that use them.
  • Cumulative cash is your running balance in the model.
  • Available cash is cumulative cash minus the reserve you chose to lock. It is not a standard “free cash flow”.

In standalone V2, the base-month net profit is after business tax but before loan interest. Check what each profit figure includes before you compare it with another.

H07. How do I handle my own money, a loan and a reserve?

The calculator works out how much money you need before sales cover your costs. Then you choose where it comes from.

What the starting need is made of

  • Payments due before the first receipts.
  • The deepest cash gap in any month.
  • A protective reserve, as a share you choose.

Own money or a loan

You can cover the need with your own funds or with a loan. Where a branch offers a loan, it works like this:

  • You pay interest every month.
  • You repay the principal in one payment at the end. It is not an annuity.
  • The loan term can be longer than the sales period. A longer term adds interest. It does not add sales.

Example (illustrative scenario)

Loan 1,200 at 12% a year for 6 months. Interest each month = 1,200 × 12% ÷ 12 = 12. Total interest 72. The 1,200 is repaid in month 6.

H08. Which taxes do I enter?

You make two separate choices, and you type the rates yourself.

  1. Tax on sales: VAT, Sales Tax, or none.
  2. Business tax: on turnover, or on profit.

Sales tax passes through to the state. It is not your revenue. Sales Tax is applied to the price after the discount.

Example (illustrative scenario)

Price 100, discount 10%, Sales Tax rate 8%.

Price after discount: 90. Sales Tax: 90 × 8% = 7.20. The buyer pays 97.20. Your revenue is 90.

What the calculator does not do

  • It does not work out which rate is correct for you.
  • It does not apply individual deductions or reliefs. They depend on your country, tax regime and business. Ask an accountant.
  • It does not prepare tax returns.

An agent and a principal are taxed on different bases. Set the role to match your contract.

H09. How do currencies and the converter work?

There are two currencies to think about:

  • Business currency: the one your whole model is in. You pick it at the start.
  • Payment currency: the one you really pay a supplier or carrier in. It can differ for each payment.

Converting a foreign payment

  1. Enter the amount in the payment currency.
  2. Choose the payment currency next to it.
  3. Check the rate, its source and its date, or type your own rate.
  4. Apply it to the calculation.

Example (illustrative scenario)

A purchase of 1,000 CNY, with a rate you apply of 0.14 USD per CNY, becomes 140 USD in a USD model.

Good to know

  • Choosing a currency does not change the amount you typed.
  • A payment is converted once.
  • Old drafts keep the rate they were saved with. They are not updated when the market moves.
  • No bank fee or exchange spread is added unless you enter it.
  • If the rate source is unavailable, enter the rate by hand.

H10. How do I save, continue and start over?

Your work is saved in your browser, on the device you are using. There is no account and no cloud backup.

  • V1, standalone V2, linked V2 and the demo each keep their own saved draft.
  • Opening a demo does not overwrite your own draft.
  • Clearing your browser data, or opening the calculator in another browser or on another device, means your draft will not be there.
  • Starting a new calculation is not the same as editing a saved one. Check which one you are in before you change anything.

To keep a result, export it (H11).

The calculator runs in your browser. More on this in Data and privacy.

H11. How do I get a PDF, Excel file or standalone HTML?

PDF and Excel

  • V1 can produce a PDF or report.
  • V2 can produce a price list as a PDF and as an Excel (.xlsx) file.
  • Excel cells hold real numbers you can work with. A separate “Notes” sheet carries the warnings. The file follows the language you are using.
  • The price list shows the net price, the buyer price, the sales tax and notes. It is a price list, not an accounting ledger.

Standalone HTML

You can download the full calculator as one HTML file. It is a copy of the tool. By default it does not contain the numbers you entered. Do not confuse it with a PDF of your calculation.

The HTML file is for people who want to run the calculator themselves or put it on their own site (H12).

H13. How should a visitor prepare for a consultation?

A client who has run the numbers first has better questions. They describe their idea, enter costs, price, sales and cash limits, and bring the results.

Questions worth taking to a specialist:

  1. I've listed my costs. Is anything missing?
  2. The calculator suggests this price. How does it compare with alternatives?
  3. Who would buy this from me at this price, and how can I check?
  4. Which shared costs might I be underestimating?
  5. How much money do I need before the first sales, and how should I fund it?
  6. Which tax set-up should I use?
  7. What should I test first in a small pilot?

The calculator does not qualify leads for you, and it does not send anything to a consultant. The client brings the numbers.

H17. What can the calculator not check?

It checks whether your own numbers hold together. It cannot check the world outside them.

  • Demand and competition. It does not find buyers, work out their reasons to buy, or design your value proposition and packaging.
  • Legality. Permits, licences and rules for your business are not checked.
  • Your inputs. Prices, conversion rates and costs are only as good as what you enter.
  • Taxes. Rates, exceptions and filing dates are not checked.
  • Actual payments and results. The model is a plan.
  • Profit. A calculated result does not guarantee it.

How to test your idea

  • Ask suppliers for real quotes.
  • Talk to the people you think will buy.
  • Run a small pilot or test sale.
  • Show your numbers to an accountant or consultant.

Then change your assumptions and recalculate.

Example A. A cotton tote bag (V1)

A hypothetical small workshop in Almaty makes plain cotton tote bags in one batch. Figures are in Kazakhstani tenge (KZT). This is a scenario built from published benchmarks and stated assumptions. It is not a forecast of orders. Inputs and sources.

Open the example in the calculator (own funds). Opening a demo does not overwrite your own draft.

  1. The idea is yours. One batch of 500 sellable bags.
  2. Direct cost. Fabric, thread, label, packaging, delivery of materials and sewing time. A 3% reject rate means making 516 sets to get 500 bags. Allocated cost per sellable bag: about 1,426 KZT.
  3. Price you need. With own funds, the V1 buyer price including VAT comes out at about 5,177 KZT. Advertising cost per forecast customer: 1,667 KZT (a 250,000 monthly budget ÷ 150 orders).
  4. The whole business. Materials, production, rent, a sales contractor and advertising together make a cycle budget of about 1.91 million KZT before reserve. The reserve is about 191 thousand. Starting funding: about 2.10 million. A full month of active sales earns about 70.5 thousand after model tax. That is a 10.5% margin, not the 20% target you typed.
  5. Who, why, where? A printed cotton tote is advertised at 4,500 KZT. It is not the same product, but your calculated price is higher. Who would pay 5,177, for what reason, and where do they look? What value and packaging justify the difference?
  6. Check. At 4,500 KZT and the same 150 monthly sales, the result before profit tax and interest is only about 1,568 KZT. Test price, cost and channel with real buyers.
  7. Recalculate with what you learn.

Example B. A US retail store for 12 months (V2)

This is a separate, ready-made study in standalone V2. It is a worked example, not a promise of US demand.

Open the US store example. It keeps its own draft and does not touch yours.

  • 10 items (SKUs), sourced in three ways: made by the shop, bought for resale, and dropshipped.
  • 14 paid resources: rent, staff, equipment and others. Each is entered once and assigned to the items it serves.
  • Advertising, with a shared budget and an extra campaign for some items.
  • Sales Tax of 6.25%, shown in the buyer price. Your revenue excludes it.
  • A 12-month loan with interest paid monthly and the principal repaid at the end.
  • A monthly view of cash, stock, prices and tax effects.

Look at the difference between the margin of each item and the result for the whole store. Then look at how the loan and the stock purchases move the cash balance in ways the profit figure does not show.

The standalone V2 model has no month-by-month Sales Tax account. The tax appears in the buyer price and is kept out of revenue.

Example C. A consultant and a client

  1. The client does the first pass. On your site, they calculate the cost of their idea, the price they need, and what the business as a whole needs in money.
  2. They arrive with questions. Who would buy at this price? Why? Where? What value and packaging do I need? (See H13.)
  3. They contact you through your own channels. A form, chat or phone on your site (H15).
  4. You test the value idea with them. Compare it with alternatives and real buyers' reactions.
  5. They recalculate. A changed price, cost or channel goes back into the calculator.

For you, the setup is a free HTML file placed on your site (H12) or a White Label project (H14). The calculator does not pass the client's numbers or contact details to you.
